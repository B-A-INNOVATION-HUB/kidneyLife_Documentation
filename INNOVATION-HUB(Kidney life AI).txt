PART A: DOCUMENTATION ASPECTS
1.	KidneyLife AI’s Relevance to the Theme
The diagnosis of kidney disease depends on the timely reading of CT scans, yet in South Africa’s public hospitals it is often delayed by a shortage of radiologists and unreliable scanning capacity. Although the Fourth Industrial Revolution is widely promoted, few public-sector patients have felt its benefits. KidneyLife AI answers the theme “an AI Solution for Industries” by bringing deep learning into the healthcare industry as a decision-support tool for under-resourced hospitals such as Dr George Mukhari Academic Hospital in Ga-Rankuwa. The solution is trained on 12,446 labelled CT images from the publicly available CT Kidney Dataset and classifies each abdominal CT image as Normal, Cyst, Stone, or Tumour, with a confidence score for every prediction. Scans about which the model is uncertain are flagged for radiologist review, so clinicians can attend to urgent patients first while the final diagnosis remains with the medical professional. In this way, the solution reduces diagnostic workload, shortens the time from scan to treatment, makes better use of existing CT equipment, and shows how affordable AI can serve communities that new technology usually leaves behind.

2. Business Objectives
The objectives of KidneyLife AI are to:
Shorten the time between a CT scan and a first classification of the kidney finding to under 10 seconds per image.
Help radiologists order their worklist by flagging scans that the model predicts as Stone or Tumour.
Reduce missed kidney tumours and stones by reaching a high recall for these two classes.
Lower the cost of kidney diagnosis in public hospitals by running on ordinary laptops without specialised hardware.
Keep clinicians in control through visible confidence scores and an automatic referral warning for uncertain predictions.
Build a foundation that can later be extended to other organs, imaging types and hospitals.

3. Business Success Criteria
Overall test accuracy: To achieve at least 90\% accuracy on the unseen test set of 1,871 CT images, as reported by the model evaluation step.

Recall for Tumour and Stone: To achieve at least 90\% recall for each of these two classes, as shown by the per-class recall in the classification report.

Speed of a single prediction: To classify a single CT image and display a confidence score for each diagnosis in less than 10 seconds on a laptop CPU, timed during the live demonstration.

Speed of full test evaluation: To process all 1,871 test images and produce a confusion matrix and classification report in less than 5 minutes on a laptop with 16 GB RAM or less, timed when the report is generated.

Safety of uncertain predictions: To display a referral warning for every prediction below 80\% confidence, checked during single-image testing.

User acceptance: To have at least 80\% of pilot clinicians rate the tool as useful, measured by a questionnaire after a proposed two-week pilot at Dr George Mukhari Academic Hospital.

4. Business Background
South Africa’s public health system serves most of the population, yet it has very few imaging specialists. The country has approximately 700 active radiologists, about one per 100,000 people compared with 13 per 100,000 in Europe, and only about 20 new radiologists qualify each year (World Health Expo, 2024). Every CT scan must still be reported by one of these specialists, so scans queue up whenever demand rises or staff are absent.
Imaging capacity in public hospitals is also fragile. At Dr George Mukhari Academic Hospital, two older CT scanners stopped working during 2023, and a newer one failed in January 2024, leaving the hospital without a working CT scanner for nearly three weeks. Patients who would normally stay one day in casualty waited four to six days (Pongweni, 2024). At Charlotte Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital, about 2,600 oncology patients were waiting for MRI scans, with outpatient bookings extending to December 2026 (Bateman and Joseph, 2026). When scanning time is this scarce, every scan must be reported and acted on as quickly as possible.
Kidney disease adds to this pressure. Estimates suggest that up to 17% of South Africans may have chronic kidney disease, driven partly by diabetes and HIV, while dialysis and transplants in the public sector are strictly rationed (Chothia, 2025). Finding stones, cysts and tumours early therefore protects kidney function and avoids expensive emergency care.
We at INNOVATION-HUB recognised that artificial intelligence can relieve part of this workload. Published research on the same CT Kidney Dataset reported accuracies above 99% with deep learning models (Islam et al., 2022), which shows that automatic classification of these four conditions is achievable. We therefore developed KidneyLife AI, a practical decision-support tool that a public hospital can afford. It is a lightweight model that runs on a standard laptop, sorts kidney CT images into Normal, Cyst, Stone and Tumour, and shows the clinician a confidence score for each prediction. By automating part of the diagnostic process, it helps clinicians prioritise urgent cases and shortens the time to medical intervention


5.	Requirements

5.1 Functional Requirements
•	The system must accept CT kidney images in JPG or PNG format.
•	The system must find the dataset automatically and split each class into training (70%), validation (15%) and test (15%) sets.
•	The system must pre-process every image by converting it to grayscale, resizing it to 128 × 128 pixels and scaling the pixel values to between 0 and 1.
•	The system must classify each image as Normal, Cyst, Stone or Tumour and show the confidence score for all four classes.
•	The system must display a referral warning when the highest confidence score is below 80%.
•	The system must produce a test report containing accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and a confusion matrix.
•	The system must save the trained model and load it again without retraining.
•	The system must let the user classify either a random test image or their own image by entering its file path.

5.2 Non-Functional Requirements
•	The system will complete a single prediction in less than 10 seconds on a laptop CPU.
•	The system will use a fixed random seed so that the data split and training give consistent results on repeated runs.
•	The system will use plain-language prompts and messages so that non-programmers can operate the tool.
•	The system will run on any Windows laptop with Python 3.10 to 3.13 without the user editing file paths.
•	The code will be organised into eight clearly commented parts.
•	The system will process images locally, and the images will contain no patient names, in line with the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA).
•	The system will stop with a clear message, rather than crashing, when the dataset or a class folder is missing.


6.	Constraints

•	Scope: The system analyses only two-dimensional abdominal CT kidney images in four classes (Normal, Cyst, Stone and Tumour). Other organs, other imaging types (X-ray, MRI and ultrasound) and full 3D CT volumes are out of scope.
•	Data origin: The training data comes from hospitals in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The model’s performance on images from South African scanners and patients has not yet been validated.
•	Hardware: Training is done on laptop CPUs, because TensorFlow has not supported GPUs on native Windows after version 2.10 (TensorFlow, 2026). Images are therefore reduced to 128 × 128 grayscale, and training is limited to 20 epochs. Our first training run took about 20 minutes, at 87 to 144 seconds per epoch.
•	Clinical information: The model sees only the image, not the patient’s history, symptoms, or laboratory results.
•	Regulation: KidneyLife AI is an academic prototype. It is not a registered medical device and may not be used for clinical decisions without regulatory approval.

7. Risks

7.1 A tumour or stone is classified as Normal

Likelihood: Medium
Impact: High
Mitigation:
Class weights make errors on rare classes cost more during training.
Recall per class is monitored in every test report.
Predictions below 80% confidence are referred to a radiologist.

7.2 Dataset bias (images from one country)

Likelihood: High
Impact: Medium
Mitigation:
Data augmentation adds variety in angle, position and contrast.
Validate on anonymised South African images before any pilot and retrain with local data.

7.3 Clinicians rely on the tool too much

Likelihood: Medium
Impact: High
Mitigation:
Clear statement that the tool supports and does not replace diagnosis.
Confidence for all four classes is always shown.

7.4 Breach of patient privacy

Likelihood: Low
Impact: High
Mitigation:
Use only anonymised images and process them locally (POPIA).
Role-based access control in any future deployment.

7.5 Laptop too slow or training interrupted

Likelihood: Medium
Impact: Medium
Mitigation:
Small grayscale images and early stopping shorten training.
The best model is saved after every improvement and can be reloaded.

7.6 Low user adoption

Likelihood: Medium
Impact: Medium
Mitigation:
Two-week pilot with training sessions.
A radiologist champion and a short user guide.

9.	Problem Definition
What is the problem?
Kidney cysts, stones and tumours are usually confirmed with abdominal CT scans, and a radiologist must report every scan. South Africa has roughly one radiologist per 100,000 people (World Health Expo, 2024), and public hospitals feel this shortage most. When scanners fail or demand rises, the queue grows: at Dr George Mukhari Academic Hospital, patients waited four to six days in casualty during a CT outage (Pongweni, 2024). At the same time, up to 17% of South Africans may have chronic kidney disease (Chothia, 2025). When CT images wait to be read, a tumour or blocking stone can be found late, leading to lost kidney function, emergency surgery or a poorer cancer outcome.
How will solving it help?
KidneyLife AI classifies each kidney CT image within seconds and shows a confidence score for every prediction. Radiologists can review suspected tumours and stones first, so waiting times should fall and patients in under-staffed public hospitals reach treatment sooner, while the doctor still makes the final diagnosis. For the Ga-Rankuwa community and the wider City of Tshwane, this means shorter casualty stays, fewer emergency admissions and better use of existing CT scanners, which lowers pressure on the public health budget. The solution runs on a standard laptop, so other public hospitals could adopt it without expensive hardware.
